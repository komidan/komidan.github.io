Flashy websites with custom animations, 3D effects, and tons of interactable
features are cool and can showcase creativity and ingenuity, but do regular
websites _really_ need to be so demanding on hardware? Some websites I visit run
fine on my beefed-up gaming system with a dedicated GPU, but my laptop from 2016
with an iGPU is a completely different story. Pages take upwards of 3-10 seconds
to load all the content, Javascript, and render the intensive CSS animations.

This is not meant to dog on websites where the point _is_ to be elaborate and
flashy. That's a medium for creativity itself. Just look at all the Neocities
websites people have made. They are all cool, and both display a personality as
well as a sense of nostalgia from the old days of the internet.

I believe modern technology (web included) that is designed to be used by many -
meaning it's not just a showcase piece, should be optimized for lower-end
hardware. They should be capable of running on 10 year old systems relatively
easily. Especially in this economy...

After having found a new appreciation for simplicity, I wanted to deepen my
understanding of what _**simple**_ really means. I went and found some websites
that are on the simpler side, yet some still feature things that can make them
not exactly, _simple_.

> **DISCLAIMER**
> These reviews are based on what they looked like in November 2026.

- **https://hayyoth.org/** \
  This is the website of a recently popular YouTuber who definitely embodies the
  very concept of "simple". His website has changed a bit since I first saw it,
  but both the original and newest iteration reflect what I initially liked
  about it: very little information, and barely any CSS at all. This
  is the type of website that could be on the side of _too_ simple, where it
  becomes less intuitive because people are more used to modern websites. This
  website appears easily maintainable, and editable in that it just requires
  very basic HTML knowledge over some massive tech stack. True simplicity that
  lets the page load quickly, and nothing is in the way.

- **https://brittanychiang.com/** \
  This website is made by a front-end engineer. It's very clean, yet _still_
  simple. There are no overly-distracting attention stealers, big animation
  displays that obstruct viewing, and the content flows nicely. The
  cursor-following glow is a nice touch, it is a cool addition that stays out of
  the way. This website is simple and showcases what Brittany wanted to
  showcase - nothing more. I would talk about more but it _is_ very
  simple. Maybe that's a bad thing depending on her goal, but if simplicity was
  a key motivator, it's succeeded.

- **https://www.taniarascia.com/** \
  This one is interesting to me because initially I thought it was too complex
  because of all the text and a large empty side bar with a bunch of buttons at
  the top and bottom. After exploring for a bit though, I changed my mind
  slightly. This website gives a strong feeling of a programming language's
  documentation page: a lot of words, but still makes sense (usually). It does
  have some stand-out flashy colors in places and some embossed cards with a
  drop shadow, however they are _functional_ styling on links or border
  transitions to hint at a clickable box.

- **https://arpitbhayani.me/** \
  Another website with minimal unnecessary flashy effect, just functional
  transitions and scale changes to hint at clickable areas. This has _a lot_ of
  text though, very overwhelming to someone who is just passing by wanting to
  see who this person is. I suppose this could be classified as _informational
  complexity_? The navbar getting cut off by a `max-width` property is a little
  weird to me too, but that's nit-picking. This is a simple site, but on the side
  of _too much_ content to navigate.

So, after viewing these websites and trying to figure out how to categorize
them, I've concluded that each website can be ranked by their level of visual,
functional, and informational complexity. So, what do each of these
categories mean?

### Visual

The _visual_ complexity of a website is about the appearance and presentation.

Specifically:

- Animations, transitions and other visual effects.
- Color palettes, gradients, shadows, and borders.
- Typography, spacing, and the layout.
- Depth, layering, and positioning of elements.
- Decorative elements that contribute to a website's personality.

Visual complexity is not a bad thing depending on how it is framed. Some of the
neocities pages I've seen are all over the place, and have effects that simulate
a cyberpunk environment with neon colors and many other themes. The complexity
defined here is how _easy_ the page is to understand even with those elements.

### Functional

Functional complexity deals with how the users interact with the website and how
functional elements are designed. Is it immediately obvious it's a button, or
clickable element?

Specifically:

- Navigation bars, menus, and links.
- Buttons, dropdown menus, search fields.
- How information is organized, and accessed (different web pages, or sections
  on a page).
- Whether the website shows additional functionality beyond displaying the main
  content.
- How intuitive the navigation is.

Websites with a lot of functionality can be overwhelming, but it can be spread
across different pages or accompanied by visual clues that indicate buttons or
clickable elements. Often, I prefer just putting text on the
screen because it is easier than trying to understand an icon or image.

### Informational

The informational complexity of a website is about the density and the total
amount of information presented.

Specifically:

- The amount of written content (e.g. word count).
- How densely packed the information is (rather than visual density, such as line-height).
- How that information is abstracted into categories or other pages.
- Whether the content is easy to scan/skim and still pick up relevant information (which is more about writing mechanics I guess?).
- How much effort is required to _find_ the information someone wants.

A good example of this is a short, concise README on a GitHub page. I have seen
projects where you scroll over a 1,000 lines just to find the section you want.
I mean, GitHub has a wiki feature! This issue applies to websites too. Don't
force a user to scroll, scroll, and scroll. Make links to other pages, or a
table of contents.

## Reviewing the Websites, again.

Okay, so now that you have a better understanding of how to evaluate each
website, I'll review them again and rank each category with brief explanation.

- [Hayyoth](https://hayyoth.org/)
  - **VISUAL**: Nonexistant.
  - **FUNCTIONAL**: Very low complexity. There are only links and text, not even
    a navbar.
  - **INFORMATIONAL**: Very low complexity. The ONLY issue I have with this
    entire website is that the table's info row was a little hard to understand
    at first.

- [Brittany Chiang](https://brittanychiang.com/)
  - **VISUAL**: Low complexity. Not a large color palette, there is a gradient
    that follows the cursor, and the layout makes sense with three distinct
    visual sections.
  - **FUNCTIONAL**: Low complexity. There is a table of contents for the content
    section, links are effectively placed on the bottom left. Content moving
    off-screen makes it clear you should scroll.
  - **INFORMATIONAL**: Medium complexity. There is quite a bit of text
    immediately thrown at your face, but it's sectioned nicely and allows you
    to read only what you are interested in reading.

- [Tania Rascia](https://www.taniarascia.com/)
  - **VISUAL**: Medium complexity. There are cards that add depth, some fun
    images and icons/emojis next to links that add character.
  - **FUNCTIONAL**: High complexity. There are a lot of links that are not
    immediately obvious, but still make sense to be links based on surrounding
    context. There is a navbar on the left side with social buttons and more
    links at the bottom of the bar. There are duplicated links in places that
    end up just giving you a round-trip sometimes. Some headings like "Latest"
    on the home page is confusing, latest _what_? It's only clear once you read
    more. The "Shelves" are an interesting concept, but I had to click the link
    to understand what they were really. Icons are not always clear and
    consistent.
  - **INFORMATIONAL**: Low complexity. There are clear, understandable tags
    (besides "Shelves" initially) that make sense. There also isn't a whole lot
    of text on the home page making skimming easier.

- [Arpit Bhayani](https://arpitbhayani.me/)
  - **VISUAL**: Low complexity. There is only one image, a selfie. Other than
    that, the styling is primarily used to signify clickable elements. There are
    distinct visual sections between elements.
  - **FUNCTIONAL**: Medium complexity. There are many options in the navbar, as
    well as at the bottom of the main page. Most of the pages themselves are
    organized nicely and the buttons make sense based on the name.
  - **INFORMATIONAL**: High complexity. This website has _so_ much text and
    information. Arpit has written many books, papers, and also has
    instructional videos and courses. It is a lot to take in, but just shows how
    passionate he is about the world of tech. Even though there is a lot of
    information, finding it is relatively simple. It can, however, be lengthy
    reads or videos.

## Final Thought

As with most things, creating a website is a balancing act. Finding a good
middle ground between visual elements, functionality, and the amount of
information to present is, apparently, pretty difficult. Even _this_ website
will probably receive small changes and updates to it over time as I refine
my concept of simplicity, and decide to change CSS elements occasionally.
